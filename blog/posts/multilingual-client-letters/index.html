Writing to Clients in Their Own Language: AI for German, French and English Letters

LanguagesCorrespondenceClients

A notary in Fribourg drafts a contract for a client who has always written in French. A doctor in Biel answers a patient in whichever language the patient prefers. A fiduciary in Lugano prepares a report that a client in Zurich will read. In Switzerland, writing well means writing in the client's language — and getting it slightly wrong reads as carelessness.

This article describes a simple routine for client correspondence in German, French and English. The AI helps you draft the message in the client's language so it reads naturally, not like machine translation. You stay in control of every word and every tone. No new software project — just a short prompt and a little care before you press send.

Why the client's language matters

Language in Switzerland is not just a means of communication; it is a form of respect. A client who receives a letter in their own language feels understood. A client who receives a clumsy translation quietly wonders what else was handled carelessly.

  • Trust is local: a fiduciary who writes fluent French to a client in Geneva signals that the relationship matters.
  • Small mistakes read loudly: one wrong gender, one anglicism, one sentence translated word for word, and the client starts to doubt the details they cannot see.
  • Tone does not translate on its own: a polite Swiss-German phrase does not always become a polite French one. The AI helps you carry the meaning, not just the words.

A three-step routine

Step 1 — Write the message in your own words

Start in the language you think in. Write down what you want to say, roughly, without worrying about elegance. The AI's job is the language, not the content — the facts, the numbers and the decisions stay yours.

Here is what I want to tell my client. Rewrite it in clear, polite French (Swiss French, informal « tu »). Keep the meaning and the structure, but make it sound as if a real person from this region wrote it. Do not add anything I did not say. Avoid anglicisms — use « réunion », « offre », « facture », « clients ».

Step 2 — Ask for natural phrasing, not a translation

This is the step that separates a useful draft from a wooden one: ask the AI to write, not to translate. Translation produces literal, stiff sentences. Asking the AI to write the way a native speaker would produces a message a real client would not even notice.

Read this German letter I drafted. Rewrite it in natural Swiss German (informal « du », no ß, no anglicisms — « Offerte », « Sitzung », « Pendenzen », « Kunden »). Keep it no urgency, no « sofort ». Preserve all the facts and the friendly tone.

Step 3 — Read it back in your own language

Before you send, ask the AI to translate the finished message back into your own language. This « back-translation » is your safety net: you read it to confirm that nothing was added, dropped or subtly changed. If the meaning survived the round trip, the letter is ready.

What the AI is good at — and what it is not

An AI is genuinely helpful here: it carries tone, chooses phrasing a native speaker would recognise, and remembers to avoid the anglicisms that quietly mark a text as foreign. It is patient and never gets tired of drafting the same polite paragraph twice.

It is not a substitute for your judgement. It does not know your client's history, your private shorthand, or the legal nuance behind a sentence. And it cannot know what you deliberately left unsaid. Treat it as a careful colleague who writes well in three languages but decides nothing.

A note on data

Client letters contain names, amounts and sometimes health or legal context, so the usual data-protection rules apply. Use a business-grade tool rather than a private consumer account, check whether the provider stores your input or trains on it, and anonymise first if you are unsure — replace names and figures with placeholders, draft the message, then add the real details back at the end.

The outcome

The goal is a letter your client reads without noticing the language — which is, quietly, the highest compliment a Swiss professional can receive. Start with the next message to a client in another language region. Three steps, one prompt, and every client hears from you in their own words.
