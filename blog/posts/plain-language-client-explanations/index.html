Explanations Your Clients Actually Understand: An AI Routine for Plain-Language Advice

ClientsCorrespondenceLanguages

There is a moment in almost every client conversation where you can see it happen: you explain something you understand deeply, and the client's eyes glaze over. A notary describes a purchase-contract clause, a fiduciary explains a tax ruling, a doctor walks through a lab result — and the person across from you nods politely while understanding almost none of it.

The trouble is rarely the client. It is the distance between what you know and what they need to know. The good news is that closing that distance is a skill, not a talent — and it is one a calm AI routine can help you practise, one explanation at a time.

Why your expert language gets in the way

When you work with something every day, its language becomes invisible to you. Terms that feel precise and efficient — "restriction of the right to alienate", "deferred tax liability", "paroxysmal" — are simply noise to the person hearing them once. This is the classic curse of knowledge, and it has real consequences:

  • Clients do not act. A client who does not understand an explanation does not follow the advice in it, however sound that advice is.
  • Clients come back with the same question. An unclear explanation generates a second phone call and a third email, each one eating into your week.
  • Clients lose trust. People do not confuse clarity with incompetence; they confuse confusion with it. When they cannot follow you, they quietly wonder whether you can.

What plain language actually means

Plain language is not dumbing down, and it is not stripping out the facts. It is saying the same true thing in a way the reader can follow on the first pass. The difference is usually a few small habits:

  • One idea per sentence. Long sentences with three clauses are the main reason people reread a paragraph and still miss the point.
  • Short words over long ones. "Use" instead of "utilise", "end" instead of "termination", "about" instead of "approximately".
  • Concrete examples. An abstract rule becomes clear the moment you attach a number or a name to it.
  • A structure the eye can follow. A short heading, a few clear paragraphs and a short list beat a wall of text every time.

A calm AI routine for explaining clearly

You do not need to become a copywriter. You write your explanation the way you always would, and let the AI do the slow work of making it readable. In three unhurried steps:

Step 1 — Write the raw version

Put your explanation down exactly as you would say it. Do not try to simplify while you write; that is what the second step is for. The goal here is to capture the facts and the reasoning, nothing more.

Step 2 — Hand it to the AI with the audience

Tell the AI who is reading and what they need to leave with:

Here is my explanation for a client: [your raw text]. Rewrite it in plain language for a Swiss [notary client / fiduciary client / patient / small-office client] with no legal or medical background. Keep every fact, number and deadline exactly as it is. Use short sentences, one idea each, and replace jargon with everyday words. Structure it with a short heading and a few short paragraphs. Do not add advice I did not give.

Step 3 — Review for accuracy, not style

Read the result once, looking for one thing only: has any fact changed? Numbers, dates, obligations and warnings must stay identical. The AI is very good at simplifying; your job is to make sure the simplification did not simplify the truth.

A second prompt: the follow-up question

The best test of an explanation is not how it reads, but what the client asks next. Before you send it, ask the AI to play the client for a moment:

Read this explanation the way a client with no specialist background would: [your plain-language draft]. What is the first question a client would ask after reading it? Is there any sentence that is still confusing or could be read two ways? List up to three improvements that would make it clearer, and rewrite only those sentences.

One honest caveat

Plain language must never change the meaning. For legal, tax and medical explanations, the simplified version has to say exactly what the careful version says — the clarity is in the words, not in leaving things out. You remain responsible for every fact, and the AI is there to help you say it better, not to say less.

The outcome

After a few weeks you will notice the pattern: fewer second calls asking the same question, more clients who actually do what you advised, and a reputation for being the person who explains things clearly. That is the quiet, compounding payoff of a small routine — and it costs you one draft, one prompt and one careful read-through.
