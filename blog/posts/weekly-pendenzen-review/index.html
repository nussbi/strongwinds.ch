The Weekly Pendenzen Review: 20 Minutes for a Clearer Week

PlanningWorkflowFocus

Every week the same quiet backlog appears: the reply you promised, the document half-finished, the small task that "can wait until tomorrow". In a Swiss practice — a notary's office, a fiduciary, a doctor's surgery — these Pendenzen (open items) are rarely dramatic. They are simply always there.

This article describes a simple weekly routine: twenty minutes, once a week, in which you clear the open items so they stop following you around. The AI does the mechanical sorting; you do the deciding. No software project, no big setup — just a habit that gives you back your week.

Why open items pile up

The open items themselves are not the problem. The problem is that they live in different places — the inbox, the notebook, a sticky note, your head — and because each one is small, it gets postponed. Individually they cost almost nothing. Together they become a permanent background hum.

  • They live in different places: one sits in the inbox, one on a slip of paper, one only in your memory.
  • They are never urgent — until they are: the "small" task that suddenly blocks a client mandate.
  • They follow you home: the open loop that keeps you half-thinking about work in the evening.

The 20-minute weekly review

The routine has three steps and fits into the last twenty minutes of the week — a Friday afternoon is a good place for it. The point is not to do everything, but to see everything and decide.

Step 1: Collect everything onto one list

Gather the open items from wherever they live: the inbox, the notes, the half-written drafts, the "next week" pile. Let the AI merge them into a single list of Pendenzen.

Here are my open items from this week — emails, notes and drafts below. Merge them into one list. For each item, give a short title, say who it is for, and mark whether it is a reply, a decision or a task. Leave out duplicates. Do not invent anything.

Step 2: Sort by "what happens if I leave it"

Not everything on the list deserves your attention. Let the AI sort the items by consequence: what happens if this waits another week? Then mark each one — do now, delegate, schedule, or drop.

Sort this list by consequence. For each item, note in one line what happens if it waits another week, and suggest one of: do now, delegate, schedule, drop. Stay neutral and factual — no urgency.

Step 3: Draft the replies and schedule the rest

For the items that need a reply, let the AI draft it — factual, no pressure. For the items that need time, put them on the calendar. The goal is that nothing stays "somewhere at the back of your mind".

Draft a short, friendly reply for these three items. Tone: factual, no urgency. Also suggest a date next week for the two tasks that need time.

Why this works — and what stays human

The review works because it is small and repeatable. You are not reorganising your whole office; you are giving twenty minutes to a single habit. Over a few weeks the open items stop accumulating, because they are caught early instead of carried in your head.

What stays human is the decision about what matters. The AI never sets your priorities; it only makes the list visible and does the typing. You decide who needs an answer today, which task can wait, and which one can be dropped entirely.

Data protection — a short note

The list often contains client names and matters, so the usual rules apply. Use a business-grade tool rather than a private consumer account, check whether the provider stores your input or trains on it, and anonymise first if you are unsure — replace names with placeholders, work out the structure, and add the real details afterwards.

Start this Friday. Twenty minutes, one list. The first week you will simply see everything that has been floating around. From the second week on, less and less will pile up — because you are no longer holding it all in your head.
