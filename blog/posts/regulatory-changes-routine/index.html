New Rules Without the Stress: An AI Routine for Circulars, Laws and Deadlines

ComplianceDeadlinesPlanning

Every few weeks, something changes. A new circular from the tax administration, a revised law, a professional guideline, a deadline that quietly crept closer. For a notary, a fiduciary, a doctor or a small office, staying current is not optional — it is part of the job. But it should not eat your evenings.

Most professionals handle it the same way: a pile of newsletters, a few association emails, an official PDF that arrived in the post, and a vague feeling that something important may have slipped past. The reading gets postponed to "a quieter week" that never comes.

Why "I'll catch up eventually" quietly hurts

Staying current by accident is not a system. It is a gamble, and the costs show up slowly:

  • The missed deadline. A filing date, a transitional period, a new fee schedule. Missing it is rarely fatal, but it is always avoidable.
  • The outdated advice. You answer a client from memory, and the rule changed six months ago.
  • The background anxiety. A low, constant hum of "what have I missed?" that follows you home.
  • The wasted hour. Re-reading a forty-page circular in full when only two pages touch your practice.

A calm routine to stay current

You do not need to read everything. You need a small system that collects, filters and condenses — and lets you decide in one sitting. Here is a routine you can run once a month, in a single quiet hour.

Step 1 — Collect your sources in one place

Make a short list of the places that actually matter to your practice: the official newsletters, your professional association, the tax administration, a legal database, one or two specialist publications. Forward or save everything into one folder — an inbox rule that puts them all in the same place is enough.

Step 2 — Let the AI condense each one

Take one circular, newsletter or law change at a time and ask the AI to filter it for you:

Here is the latest circular from the tax administration. Summarise it for a fiduciary in Switzerland. Tell me: (1) what changed compared with before, (2) who it affects, (3) any dates or deadlines, and (4) what action I may need to take. Keep it under 200 words. Flag anything that seems unusual or urgent.

Step 3 — Keep a one-page register

For every item that actually matters, write one line in a running document: the date, the source, what changed, the deadline, and what you decided to do. Over time this register becomes your own map of the rules that govern your practice — searchable, calm, always up to date.

Step 4 — Book the review hour

Put a recurring one-hour appointment in your calendar, once a month. Feed the folder to the AI, read the condensed briefs, update the register. That is the whole system.

One quiet hour a month

The rhythm matters more than the tool. One hour a month is enough for most practices, because the AI does the heavy reading and you only decide. When something important arrives — a genuinely urgent deadline, a change that affects a current client file — you can run the same prompt immediately, outside the monthly rhythm.

Here is a second prompt, for when a change touches a client you are working on right now:

A new rule about [topic] was published this month. I am currently advising a client on [situation]. Read the summary below and tell me: does this change affect my client's situation, what should I tell them, and what should I document in the file? Be specific, and quote the relevant points.

What AI cannot do for you

The AI reads and condenses, but the judgement stays with you. It will not know the quirks of your canton, the position of your professional association, or the history of a particular client. And it will occasionally get a detail wrong — which is exactly why the register and your own review remain the last word. Treat every summary as a first draft: useful, fast, and always to be checked.

The outcome

After two or three months, the routine becomes a quiet habit. The pile of unread circulars disappears. The "what have I missed" hum goes quiet. And when a client asks about a recent change, you answer from your register, not from memory or worry. Staying current stops being a burden you carry around and becomes a small, reliable system — one hour a month, and the confidence that nothing important is slipping past.
