Getting Paid on Time: An AI Routine for Friendly Payment Reminders

Cash Flow Payments Automation

A self-employed consultant in Bern sends an invoice and waits. Two weeks pass, then a month. The work is done and the client is happy — but the money has not arrived. Writing that first reminder feels awkward, so it gets postponed again.

Unpaid invoices are one of the quietest problems in a small business. They rarely show up in a plan, and they are easy to ignore until the cash-flow gap becomes real. This article describes a simple routine for chasing overdue invoices: the AI drafts the reminders in your tone, you approve them, and the money arrives without the awkwardness.

Why reminders get postponed

Most self-employed people and small offices do not have a collections department. Reminders get written in a hurry between two jobs, or not at all. The usual reasons:

  • It feels personal: you like the client and do not want to strain the relationship.
  • It sits low on the list: the next client meeting always feels more important than an old invoice.
  • The wording is hard: too soft and it gets ignored, too hard and it gets awkward.

The result is predictable: payment terms stretch, cash flow gets thin, and the awkwardness only grows the longer you wait.

A three-step routine

Step 1 — One quiet list of open invoices

Once a week, gather the invoices that are past their due date — from your accounting tool, a spreadsheet, or a simple note. Keep it small and regular; five minutes is enough. The list only needs the client name, invoice number, amount, due date and days overdue.

Step 2 — The AI drafts, you decide the tone

Give the AI your list and a short description of how you want to sound: polite, firm, friendly but clear. The AI drafts a short reminder for each client, with the right facts already filled in. You read it, adjust a word if needed, and send.

Here are three overdue invoices. Write a short, friendly payment reminder for each one, in my usual tone: polite, clear and simple. Mention the invoice number, amount and due date, thank the client for the work together, and ask when we can expect the payment. No pressure, no threats — just a nudge.

Step 3 — Escalate gently, only when needed

If a first reminder brings no answer, the AI drafts the next step: a slightly firmer second reminder, and later a short note about the consequences of late payment — late fees and interest — always factual, never emotional.

The client below has not answered my first reminder from two weeks ago. Write a second, slightly firmer reminder. Stay polite and factual, refer to the previous reminder, and mention that I will have to pass the case to my fiduciary if it stays unpaid. No anger, no ultimatums.

Why this works in practice

  • It removes the emotion: the AI writes the awkward sentence, so you do not have to.
  • It stays consistent: every client gets the same professional tone.
  • It is fast: five minutes of review instead of an hour staring at an empty email.

What this looks like across professions

  • Self-employed and independent professionals: a small stack of invoices, cleared in one weekly pass.
  • Fiduciaries: reminders sent on behalf of clients, always in the same clear, factual tone.
  • Doctors and small practices: billing that quietly follows up instead of stalling.
  • Notaries and law firms: fee notes that get a polite nudge instead of a long silence.

The routine is the same everywhere; only the wording changes.

A note on tone and data

The tone matters as much as the words. A good reminder is warm and clear: thank the client for the work together, state the facts, and ask for a date. It never threatens and never apologises for asking. On data, keep the same rule as everywhere else: do not paste confidential details into a public chatbot. Invoice numbers and amounts are usually fine, but for sensitive clients, anonymise first and add the real names back at the end.

The outcome

One half-hour a week is usually enough to keep cash flow healthy. The reminders go out on time, the awkwardness disappears, and most clients simply pay — because a polite, clear reminder is often all it takes. You stop carrying the unpaid invoices around in your head, and you get back to the work you are actually paid for.
