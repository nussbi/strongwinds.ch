Reading the Fine Print: An AI Routine for Checking Contracts Before You Sign

Contracts Compliance Documents

Every now and then, a contract lands on your desk that you simply have to sign: a lease for a new office, a supplier agreement, the terms of a software you are about to depend on. It is written in dense legal language, it is twenty pages long, and the person on the other end is waiting. So you skim it, sigh, and sign.

For a notary, a fiduciary, a doctor or a small office, this moment is more common than anyone likes to admit. The contract is rarely a trap — but the details matter. An automatic renewal, an unclear liability clause, a notice period you overlooked: these are exactly the things that come back months later as an unpleasant surprise.

Why reviewing a contract feels heavy

Reading a contract is not hard because it is complicated. It is hard because of everything around it:

  • Legal language. Sentences that run for half a page and mean something different than they first appear to.
  • Length. The clause that matters is buried on page 14, between two paragraphs of boilerplate.
  • The waiting party. There is always a deadline, a signature, or a person waiting, and the pressure quietly pushes you to skip the fine print.

The result is familiar: you sign now and promise yourself you will read it properly next time.

A simple routine in three steps

You do not need to become a lawyer or read every word yourself. You need a quiet half hour and a simple routine that catches the things that actually bite.

Step 1 — Gather the contract and the context

Put the full contract in one place — a PDF, a scan, a clean text file — together with one or two sentences of context: who it is from, what the deal is, and what worries you. You are not reading yet; you are only preparing, so nothing important is missed.

Step 2 — Let the AI flag the risky parts

Hand the AI the text and ask it to do the mechanical part: find the clauses that commonly cause trouble and lay them out in plain language. A prompt like this does the heavy lifting:

I am a small Swiss business and I have been sent a contract to sign. Please read it and list, in plain language: (1) the duration and how it ends or renews, (2) any automatic renewal or minimum term, (3) liability and penalty clauses, (4) notice periods and termination rights, and (5) anything that seems unusually one-sided or unclear. Quote the relevant sentence for each point so I can check it myself. Do not give legal advice — just show me where to look. Contract: [paste].

Step 3 — Decide and sign in one sitting

Go through the flagged list once, mark what you want to ask about or change, and send your questions or your signature. Because the AI already found the risky parts and quoted them for you, this final pass is quick and careful instead of a guilty skim.

A second prompt for the fine print

Some contracts hide the important bits in the footnotes and annexes — an auto-renewal buried in a table, a price increase tied to an index. For those, ask the AI to trace the obligations over time:

Here is a contract I am considering: [paste]. For the next 12 months, list every date on which something happens automatically — a renewal, a price change, a deadline, a notice period — and what I would need to do to prevent or accept it. Present it as a short timeline. Do not decide for me — just clarify what is coming so I can plan with care.

What the AI does well — and what stays with you

  • Finding the needle: the one clause on page 14 that actually matters, surfaced instead of skimmed over.
  • Plain language: the legal phrasing turned into short, clear sentences you can react to.
  • Timeline: the renewal and notice dates laid out in order, so nothing expires silently.

What stays with you is the judgement: what is acceptable, what to push back on, and whether to sign at all. The AI is a careful second pair of eyes, never the decision-maker. For anything material — a lease, an employment agreement, a large liability — a human review by a professional remains the sensible step.

A note on confidentiality

A contract often contains names, figures and terms you would rather keep private. Treat it like any other sensitive business document: do not paste confidential agreements into a public chatbot. Use a local model or a zero-retention service, or anonymise the names and figures first and add them back yourself. The Swiss Data Protection Act applies here too.

The outcome

Half an hour of review replaces the guilty skim and the surprise that comes months later. You sign with your eyes open, ask for the changes you want, and keep a short timeline of what renews and when. That is the whole point: not more caution, just more clarity.
