Sorting the Day's Mail: An AI Routine for Incoming Letters

MailWorkflowOrganisation

A notary in Zurich starts every morning the same way: a stack of letters on the desk. An invoice from the cantonal tax office, a bank statement, a signed contract from a client, a reminder about a land-register deadline, and a brochure nobody asked for. Every piece needs a decision, and none of them waits.

Email gets most of the attention these days, but for notaries, fiduciaries, doctors and small offices a surprising amount of important work still arrives on paper. Official letters, supplier invoices, insurance decisions and signed documents land in the letterbox rather than the inbox — and they are easy to lose under a pile of advertising.

You do not need to answer everything at once. You need a way to turn that daily stack into a short, sorted list of what actually matters, while the mechanical reading is done for you.

Why paper mail quietly causes trouble

The problem is rarely the letters themselves. It is that they arrive mixed together and demand very different things at very different speeds:

  • Deadlines hide: a reminder with a ten-day deadline looks exactly like a routine notice until you actually read it.
  • Invoices get buried: a supplier invoice folded between two flyers can sit unnoticed for weeks.
  • Priorities blur: a client's signed contract, a marketing letter and an official notice all look alike at first glance.

The cost shows up quietly: a late fee, a missed deadline, a client who waits too long for an answer.

A simple routine in three steps

You do not need to scan, file and answer everything yourself. Three quiet steps, done once a day, are enough.

Step 1 — Photograph the stack

Open the day's post and photograph or scan the letters just as they are. Do not sort first — that is exactly the part the AI takes over. A phone camera or a small desktop scanner is all you need.

Step 2 — Let the AI read and sort

Hand the images to a secure AI setup and let it do the mechanical reading. One clear prompt is enough:

Here are the scanned letters from today's post. For each one, tell me: who it is from, what kind of document it is (invoice, official notice, client correspondence, advertising, other), whether it contains a deadline or a payment due date, and what action it needs from me. Sort the letters by urgency, and put anything that can be ignored into a separate "no action" list. Never invent details — if a date or amount is unclear, mark it "to check".

Step 3 — One short list, one decision at a time

The result is a tidy daily list: three things that need you today, two that can wait until the week, and a pile of paper that can go straight to recycling. You decide what happens to each item; the AI only prepared the reading.

From there the routine almost writes itself: the invoice goes to bookkeeping, the signed contract goes to the client file, the deadline goes into your calendar — and the brochure goes in the bin.

What AI does well — and what stays with you

  • Reading and classifying: telling an invoice from a notice, and a deadline from a greeting card.
  • Extracting the essentials: sender, dates, amounts and references, pulled into a short list.
  • Drafting the next step: a short reply, a booking note, a calendar entry.

What stays with you is every decision. The AI never files, pays or replies on its own — it reads, you act. That is the whole point of a simple routine.

A note on data and security

Incoming mail is often confidential — tax details, medical findings, client contracts. It has no place in a public consumer chatbot. Use an approved setup: a local model or a zero-retention enterprise service, so the scanned letters are processed without being stored or used for training.

The outcome

Ten quiet minutes in the morning are usually enough to turn a mixed stack of paper into a short, sorted list. Nothing important slips through, the invoices reach bookkeeping on time, and the pile on the corner of the desk stops growing. The mail still arrives every day — but it stops running your morning.
