Social Media Content That Doesn't Sound Like AI: A Practical Routine for Swiss SMEs

Social MediaMarketingVoice & Tone

The signs are easy to spot: three emoji at the end of every sentence, a hook that promises the world, and a tone nobody would use in a real conversation. Generic AI posts don’t fail because the grammar is wrong — they fail because they sound like everyone else. AI can help you post consistently, but only your own voice makes the posts worth reading.

If you run a small business in Switzerland, you probably know that posting regularly helps: new clients often check your LinkedIn or Instagram profile before they ever write to you. Yet writing posts is usually the first task that slides off the week. AI tools made producing content fast — and the feeds filled with interchangeable posts. The problem is not quantity. It is sameness.

Why generic posts quietly cost you

  • Your clients notice. A fiduciary whose posts sound like a motivational calendar is contradicting the very precision clients pay for.
  • Consistency builds trust. Your voice should be recognisable across your website, your offers and your posts. If a post sounds like a different company, something quietly breaks.
  • Feeds reward real posts. Few platforms show bland, visibly generated content to many people — and those who do see it rarely act on it.

What AI is genuinely good at

  • Turning real work into ideas. After a project, one line in your notes is enough for the AI to suggest three post angles — without ever naming the client.
  • Drafting from your own material. Give the AI two or three of your past posts or newsletters as voice samples, and it drafts in your style instead of the average internet style.
  • Keeping the calendar alive. Planning a month of posts, adapting one piece for LinkedIn and Instagram, repurposing an old newsletter — mechanical work that AI handles well.

What AI cannot supply

Three things stay human. First, your opinions: a language model averages millions of voices — your actual take on a topic is the content. Second, your cases: never paste client names, figures or documents into a consumer AI tool; anonymise first, and check what the tool does with data under the revised Swiss Data Protection Act. Third, the moment itself: a photo from the actual site, the team or the workshop beats any generated image.

A practical weekly routine

  1. Keep a running note. After real work, write one line: the topic and what surprised you. No client names.
  2. Batch once a week. Give the AI your notes plus two or three of your best past posts as voice samples.
  3. Draft in a fixed structure. One hook, one concrete example, one useful takeaway, a plain next step.
  4. Edit like a human. Read it aloud, delete the buzzwords, check every fact — then cut ten per cent.
  5. Schedule and stop. Two good posts a month, published reliably, beat twenty rushed ones.
“Write a LinkedIn post from my note below, in the voice of my example posts. One concrete example, one takeaway, no emoji lists, no buzzwords, at most 120 words. Ask me if a fact is missing.”

What to measure

Do not count likes. Track the enquiries that mention your content, profile visits and direct messages — over months, not weeks. For most Swiss SMEs the audience is small but exactly right: a handful of potential clients reading carefully is worth more than a thousand anonymous impressions.

The goal is not to sound impressive. It is to sound like the person your clients will actually work with. AI gives you the consistency; you supply the character. That combination is exactly what generic posts never have.
